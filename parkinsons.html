Parkinson's

Measured twice a year. Lived every day.

Parkinson's is scored by hand at the clinic every three to six months, with nothing measured in between. Insai reads the brain at night and the motor system by day, at home, so a therapy's effect shows up on the axis where it acts — and shows up sooner.

The gap

The instrument is a person with a clipboard

Motor symptoms are scored by hand twice a year. Rigidity is graded by feel. The non-motor symptoms — disturbed sleep, cognitive decline — reach roughly 80% of patients and are assessed by questionnaire.

So a therapy that improves sleep or cognition has no objective way to show it, and a therapy that moves the motor axis has to wait for the next visit to find out.

No digital endpoint has been qualified for Parkinson's by FDA or EMA. That is the opening.

Why both axes

Parkinson's damages the brain and the motor system. They do not move together.

Each axis has a signal that carries it, and the two dissociate. That dissociation is the whole argument for measuring both.

The brain axis, read at night
Cortical slowing during sleep predicts cognitive decline in Parkinson's (Klassen et al., Neurology 2011). We measure it directly from overnight EEG rather than asking a patient to recall how their thinking has been.
The motor axis, read by day
Tremor and movement from sensors worn on the forearm, through the day rather than during a ten-minute exam. Rigidity lives in muscle tone, which is why the sleeve carries EMG as well as motion.
One axis often moves first
A therapy may shift sleep or cognition before motor symptoms change, or the reverse. Measuring one axis and not the other is how an early effect gets missed — and how a trial reads as inconclusive when it was not.
Between visits, not at them
Nights and days across weeks, in the patient's own home. A single measurement, however good, cannot separate a real change from an ordinary bad week.
The alternatives

Everything else reads one axis continuously, or both of them once

Polysomnography does read brain, muscle and movement together — for one night, in a laboratory, scored by hand. Nobody reads both axes at home, over weeks, in diagnosed patients.

How it works

Two wearables, one record, markers you can trace

The headband is ours. The sleeve comes from our partner Paragit. Both run on the same recorder, so the signals share a clock instead of arriving from two vendors' systems with no way to line them up.

Record
The headband at night, the sleeve by day, in the patient's home. Participants keep their routine; there is no lab visit and no overnight stay.
Score
Every marker reproduces its published method to a 1e-3 tolerance before it ships. Every recording is graded for signal quality from 0 to 100, so a bad night surfaces immediately instead of quietly degrading the result.
Report
Markers delivered against the part of the MDS-UPDRS they correspond to, each carrying its method, its version, and the quality of the recording behind it.
An objective read on both axes, in the months between visits, on a schedule your protocol sets rather than the clinic's.
Where it stands

What is working, what is being validated, and what we are not claiming

A marker that looks good on a benchmark and gives a different number week to week is not a product. Here is the honest state of each piece.

Live
Sleep and brain markers
Staging, sleep architecture and spectral slowing run in the platform today, in beta at three research labs. Brain slowing repeats within a person at ICC 0.91 on a public sleep cohort.
In validation
The motor panel
Tremor reaches AUPR 0.81 across 141 subjects and five device classes, read from motion sensors rather than muscle activity. Rigidity is in analysis on our partner's Parkinson's recordings and is not yet published, so we are not quoting a number for it.
In development
The combined brain-and-body score
Whether reading both axes together beats the best single signal is an open question, and we are testing it as one. Clinical validation begins later this year in a patient study at Bispebjerg Hospital, run with Optoceutics.
Not claimed
Regulatory status, and detection
Insai is not FDA-cleared and not CE-marked, and no digital endpoint has been qualified for Parkinson's by any regulator. Nothing here detects, screens for or diagnoses Parkinson's — it measures change in people who already have the diagnosis. For Research Use Only.
Withdrawn
The markers that did not survive
Four markers passed an internal benchmark and then failed under proper controls, so we pulled them rather than ship them. We will walk you through each one and what went wrong — ask, and we would rather you heard it from us than found it in a protocol.
